## Requesting a Quote

To request a quotation, you should collect the core commercial details before you start pricing. That includes product type, quantity, packing preference, destination port, and target shipment date. If your buyer is still comparing options, ask for the minimum order quantity early so you can avoid quoting an unrealistic shipment plan.

### Information to include

When you prepare a quote request, keep the message short but complete. A clear request helps you get faster answers and reduces back-and-forth.

- Product name and specification
- Estimated quantity
- Packaging requirement
- Incoterm preference, if known
- Destination port or city
- Required documents
- Preferred shipping window

## Lead Times

Lead times depend on stock availability, packaging requirements, inspection needs, and vessel schedules. A buyer often wants a fast answer, so you should separate production time from shipping time and explain both clearly.

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="In-stock" icon="check-circle">
    
    If product is available and packed, you may only need a short window for document preparation and loading coordination.

    - Quote response: 1 to 2 business days
    - Document preparation: 2 to 4 business days
    - Loading readiness: depends on vessel schedule

  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Made-to-order" icon="settings">
    
    If you produce after confirmation, include the full manufacturing window before shipment planning begins.

    - Quote response: 1 to 2 business days
    - Production: 7 to 21 days
    - Loading readiness: after packing and inspection

  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Seasonal demand" icon="bar-chart">
    
    During busy periods, vessel space and documentation review can take longer, so you should build in buffer time.

    - Quote response: 2 to 3 business days
    - Scheduling buffer: 3 to 7 days
    - Final loading date: confirm after booking

  </Tab>
</Tabs>

<Callout kind="tip">
Always separate the commercial lead time from the vessel lead time. Buyers understand delays better when you explain each stage in plain language.
</Callout>

## Port Logistics

Port planning is easiest when you confirm the loading port, the cargo readiness date, and the document cut-off date together. If your operation is close to a major port, you can reduce transfer time and make scheduling simpler for the buyer.

### What you should confirm

| Item | Why it matters | Example |
|------|----------------|---------|
| Loading port | Determines vessel options and inland trucking | Tanjung Priok, Jakarta |
| Cargo readiness date | Sets the earliest possible pickup | 2027-03-08 |
| Export documents | Needed for customs and clearance | Invoice, packing list, COO |
| Container type | Affects loading plan and freight cost | 20 ft dry container |
| Cut-off time | Prevents missed vessel departure | 2 days before sailing |

### Shipment checks

Before handover, confirm the product count, packaging condition, marks and labels, and document consistency. If anything changes after booking, update the buyer immediately so they can adjust downstream planning.

## Shipping Coordination

Your shipment coordination process should keep the buyer informed at every step. The best practice is to share one status update after packing, one after export booking, and one after vessel departure.

### Recommended coordination flow

```mermaid
graph LR
    A[Inquiry] --> B[Quotation]
    B --> C[Order Confirmation]
    C --> D[Packing and Documents]
    D --> E[Loading]
    E --> F[Shipment Update]
```

### What to share with the buyer

- Confirmed order quantity
- Packing status
- Document status
- Estimated loading date
- Vessel name and sailing date, when available
- Tracking or bill of lading reference, when issued
